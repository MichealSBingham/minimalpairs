# First speaking test

Use this protocol after the offline comparison. It tests whether the candidate measurements survive ordinary recording conditions; it does not assume that requested words were pronounced correctly.

## What the learner will do

Listen to the two sounds, record one word, replay it, and see a simple comparison of attempts. Start with *ship/sheep*. Show the word labels prominently; IPA /ɪ/ and /i/ can be revealed as an explanation. The screen should answer “what sound did I produce?” and “did my next attempt change?” before offering a correction.

Keep the speaking interaction to one clear record control, reference replay, and the two most recent attempts. If measurement fails, show a short reason and let the learner retry. Do not freeze a previous dot as if it described the current sound, draw across silence, or manufacture a percentage score. Do not call chart position an exact tongue measurement.

## Recordings to collect

Begin with 6–10 consenting adults as a failure-discovery set, including varied habitual pitch and language backgrounds. This is a practical starting sample, not a statistically powered validation study. Keep recordings local unless separately authorized for an identified service.

- Three natural repetitions each of *ship/sheep*, *sit/seat*, and *bit/beat*.
- A second real microphone or device for a subset of the same words, with device identity recorded.
- A few comfortable higher- and lower-pitched repetitions while trying to keep the vowel the same; do not request strained speech.
- Silence, room noise, a false start, two words instead of one, and a quiet attempt.
- Save *fill/feel* and *slip/sleep* for subsequent transfer testing. Do not use these as proof of learning until an actual pre/post comparison is run.

Record prompt, anonymous speaker ID, device, sample rate and attempt number separately from the audio. Preserve the original recording. Related attempts stay in the same evaluation group; changing devices does not create a new independent speaker.

## What to review independently

A listener should hear each recording without the requested word and identify which vowel they hear, allowing “ambiguous” and “unusable.” A second reviewer can check a subset and disagreements. A phonetically trained reviewer should inspect suspicious formant tracks and vowel intervals. Keep acoustic labels separate from vowel identity and acceptability.

The estimator receives audio, not the intended answer. After measurement, the assessment layer may compare the observed sound with the intended contrast. Avoid coaching wording until that assessment agrees sufficiently with independent listeners on recordings excluded from tuning.
